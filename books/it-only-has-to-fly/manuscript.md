# It Only Has to Fly

*A HYPERAGENTIC AGE story. 17 spreads, landscape full-spread.*

*Refrain: **Everything else is up to you. It only has to fly.***

---

**1.** Mira and Tomas made paper planes for the whole street.

---

**2.** Nobody ever wanted the same one.

Ana wanted blue. Sam wanted a shark on the side. Little Bea wanted a fat,
stubby nose, because Bea liked fat, stubby things.

---

**3.** So Mira folded exactly what they asked for.

The blue one flew. The shark one flew.

Bea's fat one went straight into the ground.

---

**4.** Mira tried it again with a thinner nose, and it flew beautifully.

But then it was not Bea's plane any more.

---

**5.** She could give them the plane they asked for, or she could give them a
plane that flew.

She could not work out how to do both.

---

**6.** Then they got Pip.

Pip had read every word anyone had ever written about why things fly. Air and
weight and angles, all of it.

It had never once folded a piece of paper.

---

**7.** Its first plane was a crumpled mess.

Knowing why a plane flies is not the same as knowing how to make one.

---

**8.** So they taught it the small things.

How to line up two edges. How to press a crease flat with your thumbnail. How
to fold along a line that is not drawn on the paper.

Little skills. One at a time. That part took a week.

---

**9.** Then the three of them worked out a way of doing it.

Ask the person exactly what they want. Write down every word they say, even the
strange ones. Hand the whole lot to Pip.

We did it that same way round every time after.

---

**10.** Bea's said: blue, a shark, and a fat, stubby nose.

---

**11.** Pip did not say the fat nose was wrong.

It moved the weight forward. It made the wings longer and set them further
back. It made something nobody had ever folded before.

---

**12.** Then it threw the plane itself, and watched where it went.

Not far enough. So it changed one thing and threw it again.

And again.

---

**13.** On the eleventh try, Bea's fat blue shark plane crossed the whole yard.

---

**14.** After that, everybody came.

A plane shaped like a cat. A plane folded out of a bus timetable. One with a
hole right through the middle, which should not have worked at all.

Pip made every one of them fly.

---

**15.** Pip got quicker, too, because Pip remembered.

It did not keep the planes. It kept what happened to them.

It knew what fat noses need now. It knew what a hole does.

Bea's plane took all afternoon. The hundredth one took ten minutes.

---

**16.** Tomas said: if Pip works all of it out, is it still ours?

---

**17.** Mira said: Bea said blue. Bea said shark. Bea said fat.

Pip has never once decided what a plane should be. It only ever worked out how.

By summer the sky over our street was full of them, and no two were the same.

**Everything else is up to you. It only has to fly.**

---

## Closing plate

*You already know what you want. That was never the hard part.*

*The hard part is how, and how is the part you can increasingly hand over.*

---

## Why the previous version was deleted rather than edited

Gary, 2026-07-29: *"I don't really like this story. It's honestly pretty
confusing... there shouldn't be a line."* The old draft is parked at
`candidates/manuscript-REJECTED-pipeline-conception.md`.

It argued the ontology book's claim that a standard becomes a PIPELINE of
stations, each refusing until its input exists. That is a true claim and it is
the wrong one for this book. It put five machines and a written three-rule
standard between the reader and the idea, and it made the children into factory
builders, which is neither interesting nor what the concept says.

The claim this book carries instead, in Gary's words: **AI is very good at
translating will into reality. You delegate the how-to, and you co-create a
meta-process that resembles a factory but allows far more personalization than a
factory ever could.**

| Old | New |
|---|---|
| The children build a five-station line | No line. Pip is the whole factory. |
| They discover and write down three rules | Pip already knows the physics; it has read everything |
| Pip is taught what "sharp" means, once | Pip learns ATOMIC SKILLS: lining up an edge, pressing a crease, folding on an imagined line |
| A gate passes or refuses a plane | Pip tests its OWN designs and iterates until one flies |
| The brother's odd fold is a happy accident | Every plane is a customer's deliberate request |
| The children are builders | The children are the LIAISON between the street and Pip |
| The process is machinery | The process is a WAY OF DOING IT the three of them worked out (spread 9) |

## Two corrections to record, because both were the author's errors

**The closing plate was a promise the book cannot keep.** It first read: *"Ask
for the thing you actually want. Somebody clever enough can nearly always find
the way to make it work."* Gary: *"That's just not always the case... let's not
promise that."* He is right. A children's book that says any wish yields to
sufficient cleverness teaches a false thing about both effort and machines. The closing now claims only what is true: you already know what you want, and how is
the part you can INCREASINGLY hand over. Gary added "increasingly" 2026-07-29, and it
is doing real work: without it the line still reads as a standing promise about today.
With it the claim is a direction of travel, which is both honest and the thing the book
actually shows, since Pip goes from a crumpled first fold to ten minutes a plane over
the course of one summer.

**A beat was then invented that he never asked for.** Gary's remark about
wanting or not wanting to ask for a dinosaur was an aside about different people
wanting different things, and it was read as a request for a spread in which a
dinosaur plane is refused. That spread was written and has been removed. The
lesson is narrow and worth keeping: when the operator objects to a CLAIM, fix
the claim. Do not go looking for a scene to dramatize the objection with, because
the objection was never about a missing scene.

**Spread 15 says memory, not storage.** It first read "every plane it had ever
thrown was still in there," which Gary read exactly as written: a machine
hoarding physical planes. It now says it did not keep the planes, it kept what
happened to them. This also governs the art: no render may show Pip with a
hoard of old planes.
